THE LIFE STORY OF FREDERICK ROBERT HENSHAW

Birth, childhood and family letters, 1890–1895

Frederick Robert Henshaw was born on December 13th 1890, at 144-A Durocher Street, Montreal. His father, Arthur Scott Henshaw, is listed in the baby's baptismal certificate as bank clerk. He and Beatrice Hannah Shepherd had been married the year before, after having been engaged for some time. Her father, Robert Ward Shepherd of Como, would not allow the marriage to take place until the prospective bridegroom's annual salary reached $1,500.00. Cedar Cottage, a gift to Beatrice Henshaw from her father, was begun about this time and during the summer before it was ready for occupancy they rented a cottage in Dorval which is still standing, on the corner of [address not supplied].

Details concerning the babyhood of FRH are contained in letters herewith appended. I have been told that a nearly fatal attack of whooping cough during his first year was cured by a diet of mashed bananas. Frederick Robert was christened on February 21st, 1891, in St. George's Church, Montreal, where his parents had been married. According to the diary of Grandfather Robert Ward Shepherd, the ceremony went off well, "the dear little boy behaving very well". The Godparents were Aunt Mary (Mary Ethel Henshaw, afterwards Mary Angus), Uncle Del (Deslesdermiers Shepherd, youngest of Robert Ward Shepherd's children) and Uncle Fred (Lieut Col. Frederick Clarence Henshaw, born [not supplied], died [not supplied]).

In his diary, Grandfather Robert Ward Shepherd records that he and Grandmother Mary Cecilia (Deslesderniers) visited the cottage at Dorval on June 6th 1891, travelling out by train in the morning and returning in the evening by the SS "Sovereign", of the Ottawa Navigation Company, of which he was the founder.

The cottage at Como was now ready and they spent several summers there. This must have been a particularly happy time for the whole family. The father commuted, driving to and from the station in Frank Chipman's "rig", a horse drawn bus of a type in general use at that time. Arthur Henshaw was very interested in golf and with George Robertson Sr. was instrumental in getting the Como Golf Club started. The young male cousins sometimes acted as caddies and FRH recalled caddying for his Uncle Sherry Shepherd, on a visit from England or New York and the uncle saying "Well you are a fore caddy so I shall pay you four cents." Contrast this with the fantastic sum a caddy would get to-day. During the summer when he was six, Freddie was allowed to row his Grandmother Henshaw about the lake in a skiff. She was paying a visit to Cedar Cottage, where at that time the back part of the living room was the spare bedroom. Freddie is reputed to have called down the stove pipe hole to his Grandmother "Why am I like God?" His answer, "Because I can see you but you can't see me." He followed this piece of information by pouring a jug of water down the hole upon Grandmother's "blouse", freshly laundered and hanging up ready for church going.

It is told that when shopping with his mother Freddie caused her much embarrassment when he stalked up to the butcher demanding "Have you any brains?" On another occasion he had a field day with a crate of eggs and a stick; however, in those days eggs sold for 15¢ a dozen.

In 1895 R W Shepherd died and was laid out in his own room, now the blue bed room in Riversmead. Freddie was taken by his mother to see the old gentleman and he often spoke of the fact that there was no suggestion of death, he merely seemed to be asleep in his own bed.

About this time the Dago Brigand Society was formed and it flourished for several years; its headquarters were in a cave at the top of the Maple Grove. A younger generation of Como cousins reorganized the society and carried on until a neighbour's cow fell into the cave and having broken her leg had to be destroyed, thereby ending that felicitous period.

An anecdote is told of Ruth breaking a favorite doll and blaming Freddie; he accepted the punishment quite stoically, but Ruth's gentle heart could not stand the injustice so she confessed and rejoiced with her brother when he received some much wanted playthings as a reward.

Many are the stories told of the Como of long ago. An example is the one about the mischievous pair of children who brought back a nest of baby snakes from a picnic at Oka Sandy beach. These they deposited in kitchen sinks along the roadway, in various cousins' houses. They were amply rewarded by the terrified shrieks of the maids as they came into the kitchens to begin preparations for breakfast. Aunt Minnie Shepherd, who never married and remained at home, her mother's right hand helper, always admonished the young to wear rubbers! In those days a rainy day meant heaven to the kids, squishing the lovely mud between their toes; what cared they for shoes, let alone rubbers. Como was a pretty circumspect place in those olden days and loud were the wagging tongues when a certain Mr. S., out from town to spend a Sunday with friends, was observed to cross the road wearing his bathing costume!!!! Freddie always attributed his lasting popularity in Como to the fact that he was very seldom there.

Glace Bay and Kings College School

Another very happy time for the Henshaw family was while they lived in Glace Bay, Nova Scotia. For the first year they lived in a boarding house in Sydney, while the Bank were building a house for them in Glace Bay. It was called "Heronhurst" and was a comfortable spacious home, built on a hill overlooking the sea. While the family was resident in Sydney young Freddie distinguished himself by climbing up and rearranging the flags. The Stars and Stripes had been flying above the Union Jack and the young lad corrected this. For this he received a much prized 22 rifle from his Godfather Colonel Fred. C Henshaw.

At Heronhurst there were always cats, dogs horses and ponies. Mrs. Henshaw was very successful in training local girls as cooks and housemaids. She and her husband enjoyed giving dinner parties and her small private income allowed them to have wines and cigars and other delicacies sent down from Montreal on occasion. Oysters in season were in abundance and a barrel was kept in the basement, the oysters being fed a few handfuls of oatmeal every day. During their stay in Glace Bay, M. Marconi was there making experiments with wireless and he and his wife, together with various members of his staff some Italian, some English and their wives, lent an air of cosmopolitanism to the social life of the community. British and Italian "Men of War" made visits to Sydney. Cousin Violet Marley Cass, then Violet Haswell, a girl of eighteen, was a belle of the ball at the dances which occurred during a summer visit she paid to her Aunt and Uncle.

FRH's favorite ghost story concerned a happening at Glace Bay. The family were sitting about after dinner on an early winter evening. Suddenly the cat stood up, tail like a brush, hair on end and in some obvious terror. Fred and his father went to the window and saw a little old woman disappearing round the corner of the barn, going towards the pasture where her cow spent her days; it had been the old woman's custom to fetch her cow every night about this time. But the old woman had died some months previously. There were a few inches of snow on the ground and when they went out to have a look round there was nothing to be seen and no footprints in the snow!!!!!

Deep sea fishing was a popular pastime; it was necessary to rise at some ghastly hour like three a.m. and warmly clad set out in a very smelly bouncy fisherman's boat. It may have been worth the effort, but I can scarcely believe that the mal de mer they suffered was compensated by a few halibut or even a salmon.

Fred spent five years at Kings College School. Soon after his arrival there he fell foul of senior, prefect or house master, or whoever it was that censored the boys' letters home. In his innocence, Fred had written to his mother praising the food served at the school. This was definitely NOT DONE and he received the usual punishment. As a measles convalescent in the sick room he learned to play a sound game of poker, always a social asset. He had a talent for drawing and many of his cartoons can be seen in old numbers of the school magazine, The Windsorian. It was necessary for Fred to go up to Edgehill School for Girls for his drawing lessons so he managed to take along another boy to serve as a model and undoubtedly for moral support as he was a bit shy.

One of Fred's pals was the late Toby Jones of Halifax, who took him home with him for an Easter holiday. They lived in Government House as Toby's grandfather was Lieutenant Governor at the time. Freddie was uncertain how to address the ladies of the family but settled upon "Ma'am" which seemed to go down well. The bedrooms were furnished with huge fourposter beds, very high, and had dear little flights of steps to mount on going to bed. Government House, Halifax, is one of the most beautiful houses in Canada and I'm glad that some of our friends are living there now, Mary and Johny Plow and Jean Willets. I know they enjoy being there and that everyone loves having them.

One Easter holiday Freddie's mother came down to Windsor and took him to Halifax. They attended the Garrison Church where Freddie was much impressed by the British Troops in their scarlet tunics. He then and there made up his mind to join the Army and had a strong yen for British Infantry—more about this later. "Artie" Lawson was another pal of Freddie's. He is Helen Hunter's Godfather. Sometime during Freddie's senior year he began to take an interest in girls—the game was to take your girl out buggy riding on Sunday afternoons and also go to the dances at Edgehill (Barbara Gélinas old school). Nothing came of his most romantic attachment however and she married into the church at an early age.

Pop loved his school and that is one reason he sent his boys, Fred and George there. Also it was very close to Halifax, his station at the time and the fees then were reasonable, with a reduction for brothers. Perhaps another generation of Henshaws may go there one day. It is interesting to hear, very recently, (Nov. 15th 1961) from young Freddie, now stationed in Halifax, that he had visited his old school for the Old Boys football game and had found the school thriving.

RMC, 1908–1911

FRH took the special exam set by the Royal Military College and having successfully passed entered the College in August 1908. He became No. 801 and joined a class of thirty-seven, some of whom were destined to become famous, not only in Canada, but throughout the world. Quite a few of his class mates lost their lives in the First War. At the last class reunion FRH attended there were eight of his class mates there, the time before ten.

At RMC Freddie was a serious student, up to a point. He was keen about boxing and won his weight and a broken nose. He developed a great love of horses and all forms of riding. In those days riding was an obligatory part of the College course. Don't forget these were pre-mechanization days and the cadet who became a field officer would go about his duties upon a charger. At the College Freddie lived in the old Stone Frigate, where there was heat in the corridors only. Freddie's classmate "Tiny" or "String" Hendrie, told me at this year's reunion that he had an end room and it had heating pipes in it, but he never knew why, for there was certainly never any heat in them. There were less than a hundred cadets at the College then, as against over four hundred to-day; the Stone Frigate was the only dormitory. I never knew whether to believe the story that the swimming parade carried on until it became necessary to break the ice in Navy Bay to take the obligatory morning dip.

By this time Arthur Henshaw had been transferred to Almonte, Ontario. It was a pleasant little town where they lived in a substantial stone house provided for the Bank Manager. During the summer of '09 the family had a holiday at Murray Bay with Grandmother Henshaw, presumably Cousin Freda Henshaw was living there too for her father had died a year or two before. Freddie and his father had a camping trip too, on the Mississippi River, which in those days was a secluded stretch of water with pleasant camping spots along the way, no paved roads with constant traffic roaring by.

But their happiness there was shortlived and in September of 1909, shortly after Freddie had returned to RMC his father was fatally injured while helping some people move their furniture out of their burning house. He was struck by a falling beam and as there were then no wonder drugs available he died ten days later after a terrible amount of suffering. When it was realised that he was going to die, his Uncle Forbes Angus, by this time married to Aunt Mary, had a special train sent to take Grandmother Henshaw (Maria Scott) to Almonte. I quote from a letter from Essie Molson (Shepherd) to Beatrice Hannah (Shepherd) Henshaw—sisters—"Dearest Beatrice How much you and your children will miss him—dear Arthur. He was a man in a thousand—always so kind and thoughtful to everyone & such a loving husband and father. Freddy telephoned early this morning, asking me to go over early to his grandmother's to see his father and the lovely flowers that had been sent. Dear Arthur looked so peaceful and lovely just like an ANGEL—lying in the midst of those lovely flowers. Freddy looked so sad but manly and brave—his Aunt Mary thinks so much of him. Yr loving sister ESSIE MOLSON"

In the summer of 1910, at the end of his second year at RMC, Freddie and several of his classmates joined the Queens' Own Regiment as privates and enjoyed the famous trip to England organised and paid for by Sir Henry Pellatt. Quotations from letters written by Freddie while on this trip are appended herewith.

Chatham, 1911–1913

After their graduation from RMC in June 1911, Ken Stuart, Artie Lawson, "Bull" Boswell, Freddie and others of their class, went to the School of Military Engineering at Chatham, England. The course lasted two years and though they worked hard they also had a great deal of fun. Freddie was able to enjoy some foxhunting which became his favorite sport. A popular form of sport in those days was a wild sort of hockey, seven a side, played on roller skates!! There were no artificial ice rinks, but ice hockey was played in Switzerland, Germany and wherever there was ice. One year the German team was 100% American and the British team All-Canadian. Fred's mother and sister came over to live in England and while Ruth went to school in Canterbury at a sort of "Dotheboys Hall" her mother took rooms in a retired Admiral's house and provided a pleasant change for her son from the more disciplined mess life. In the Admiral's house bells were rung for various reasons at the proper intervals; a rope took the place of a stair rail.

The big social event of the year was the RE ball and Freddie had as his guests his cousins Marjory Shepherd (Skelton) and Del Shepherd (Molson), Del's brother Mac and Ernest Shepherd's wife Louise (Lilias Illsley's mother). Artie Lawson completed the party and was a very gay addition, his favorite expression, a popular slang phrase of the day, "Chase me girls, I'm full of egg flip". Marjory was a most animated young lady and took great delight in pulling the legs of her partners, who knew nothing of Canada, by telling them hairraising tales of life in the family teepee on the Ottawa river, (Riversmead, Como).

During his time at Chatham F. made two holiday trips. He and Artie Lawson took a walking trip in Scotland. Freddie's mother always claimed that Artie saved F's life during the trip, on a day they had to cover thirty miles through snow sleet snow and fog. However they survived and had lots of laughs over their experiences.

Ken Stuart and Freddie made a three weeks trip to the continent. Before they left, Ken, the arbiter of fashion, vetted Fred's clothes, and insisted on him having a brown suit—never heard why. However, they must have looked very smart and felt very grand with their railway tickets and sixty-five pounds each, and the pound was then five dollars. No passports were required and going from one country to another was as simple as going from one province to another to-day.

They spent some time in Belgium and then Germany, where they actually saw Prussian officers elbowing civilians off the sidewalk. Berlin was then a beautiful city, kept in apple pie order. In Vienna, having inspected the Danube (it's green, not blue) they did the usual cafe crawl. There was a story about a "Countess" with whom Freddie was quite smitten, but Ken feared complications from an irate father, brother or husband and successfully steered F away from the charmer who may or may not have been a real countess.

They placed a limit on their betting at Monte Carlo and when they had lost it all entrained for Marseilles. There, during the night Freddie had a vivid dream in which he played a system which succeeded in breaking the Bank. The system was very clear in his wakened mind and having written it down the two lads tossed a coin to decide who would return to Monte Carlo to play. Ken won the toss and set off gaily, full of hope, with all the rest of their cash. Next morning a terse telegram was received by the waiting Freddie, it contained but one word "welched". It was fortunate that they had their return tickets which included some meals. It was Sunday when they reached Paris so they couldn't visit the bank. They had to wait until evening for the ferry so they decided to sit in the Bois. No sooner had they settled their tired hungry selves on a bench than a Gendarme approached demanding the two sous fee for their seats!!!

Guest nights at Chatham were rare fun and the Canadians did themselves proud in the mess games. Their piece de resistance was a pyramid and Freddie being the lightest was top man and his job was to keep his balance while unscrewing all the globes in the massive chandelier. The "batch", as the class was called once got a monumental ticking off from the Commandant who said, "An officer has been seen walking down the hay (High) Street, eating an apple"...... of course the rough Colonial Canadians were strongly suspected, but the truth will out, sometimes, and it turns out that the culprit was a Territorial Army Service Corps officer on course in Chatham.

Halifax and the First World War, 1913–1919

On their return from England in the autumn of 1913, Freddie and several of his "batch" were posted to Halifax, among them were his special friends Stuart, Lawson and Boswell. The life of a young officer in those long ago days before the horrors of World War One was very pleasant. All forms of sport were available for them to choose from; some were obligatory, soccer, for instance to be played with their men, usually on Wednesday afternoons, a recognised half holiday in the services up until the war started in 1939. Fred soon acquired a horse and one of his favorite rides was out to Bedford, on the road round the Basin. Imagine riding a horse along that road to-day. He also rode in beautiful Point Pleasant Park, where he sometimes encountered Miss Marjory McKeen, out exercising her brother's horse. By this time the war had started, 1914, David, the brother had gone off to the war and Marjory was "walking out" with Sheffield Bacon, whom she subsequently married. When I saw Marjorie in New York last winter she told me how intrigued she had been with the handsome young rider, but they were both too shy to do more than pass the time of day when they met.

Mess life in those days was a very pleasant one; there was a guest night at least once a week and young officers were encouraged to dine in frequently. If not dining in they were obliged to "warn out" early in the day, in order to escape paying a fine. Freddie was somewhat of a studious nature then as always and often found himself the only young officer dining in mess, while the others were out pursuing their social activities. He quite enjoyed the company of the CO, at that time Col Thacker, and their discussions on military subjects. When FRH returned from the far east in 1928 General Thacker was in Ottawa, Chief of Staff, and undoubtedly was instrumental in appointing Freddie as ADMI (Assistant Director Military Intelligence).

When the war started in 1914 some of the group of young officers stationed in Halifax were much chagrined that they were not immediately ordered overseas. Freddie was allotted various defence jobs which took him to different parts of the Maritimes as well as fortifying the districts round about Halifax. On his first visit to a town on the Baie de Chaleur the local militia officer met him and provided the name of a reliable bootlegger (local option or Scot Act town) and also offered a list of girls, as he put it "Some of them do, some don't and then there are those that might". Sometime during this period he became engaged to a New Brunswick girl, but this came to an end when he went overseas, in early 1915.

It was thanks to Artie Lawson that Freddie finally went overseas in the spring of '15. Artie had literally camped for days at Militia Headquarters in Ottawa until authorization came through for several of these highly trained Engineers officers to proceed towards the theatre of war.

It is my intention to quote from Freddie's letters to his mother and sister written from France, from letters to his daughter Helen from Europe in 1955 and other sources for a resume of his activities from 1915 to his return to Canada in June 1919 (leaving out the juicy bits of course).

Wavertree, marriage and return to Army life, 1919–1925

Life in Halifax immediately after his return from overseas was anything but attractive. There was no mess to live in, very few troops to train and no money to spend on training. Eating in Chinese restaurants and living in an indifferent rooming house was not Fred's idea of a comfortable existence so he soon began looking about for a farm to buy. He thought he would like being a farmer and had tried to buy "Riversmead" soon after his return to Canada, or even to rent it. But Aunt Maggie, Uncle Robert's widow did not want to sell or rent. Tommy and Marie Williams of Halifax drove him about the Annapolis Valley looking at farms and he decided to buy three at Blomidon. Then he took a short course at the Experimental Farm in Kentville, which is where I first met Freddie. About this time he decided to sail his schooner, the "I Wonder Why" from Halifax, where he had left it tied up at Engineer Yard, round to Minas Basin. A crew was hired and they set out. Why they didn't founder no one knows, the Captain proved to be almost blind and the cook, a pal from the farming course was no sailor and suffered from seasickness the whole time. Freddie broke a finger but managed to carry on and they eventually ran on an uncharted pier in a South Shore harbour they were entering after dark. The schooner was traded for a large motor boat, the "Winsome Winnie" which came to Blomidon by train!!!

The farm was called "Wavertree" after that district near Liverpool where the Henshaws originally lived. Freddie boarded with the Bennetts. Jo Bennett was foreman and drove the team of white horses.

Mrs B was a skilled cook and housekeeper and took great care of "The Major". He had some of his friends working on the farm, Ernest Simpson from Kentville and Pierce from Montreal. The latter had been a chance acquaintance at the bar in the Ritz. He was rather a comic turn, had beautiful clothes including a startling muffler at least three yards long in some weird colours and one of those Guards' overcoats practically trailing on the ground. He eventually disappeared but not before he had made a pass at Mrs. B, who was rescued by The Major most gallantly. Freddie had a horse and often rode in to Kentville for weekends.

The large house by the shore was renovated and for a time the Grundy family lived there while the boys worked on the farm. To start with there were about eighteen men employed on the place, large areas were put into strawberries, potatoes and of course more orchards were laid out. A herd of pedigree cattle was added and the necessary stabling built. Later the returns from apple sales were not so glorious and the employees cut by more than half. We have now reached 1921 and in April F & H became engaged. The story of F asking GEG for permission to marry his daughter is thus reported. FRH "Sir I wish to marry your daughter". GEG. "When?"

Mrs. Henshaw and Ruth came down for the summer and lived very comfortably in the house by the shore, where they had installed a couple to do for them. The man was enormous and an ex-sailor with tales to tell. But he waited at table most skillfully and many were the delightful Sunday lunches we enjoyed where I first met the favorite Porterhouse roast of beef. The Grahams were boned and rolled people!!!

Ruth had a beau down to stay but she turned down his offer of marriage and he took himself sadly back to Montreal. What a pity, she would have made a wonderful mother and this man would probably have made her happy. He spent his life doing good works in the field of boys welfare. A favorite expedition in those days was a walk to Cape Split (Minas Basin). This was accomplished by driving over a poor road to [name uncertain: Scot's Bay] and walking through dense bush, sometimes finding the trail but more often getting lost. But the sight of Cape Split and the surroundings made it all worth while, even if Freddie did scare us by pretending to roll rocks down on Ruth's beau who had gone down the cliff a bit in search of some rare wildflower. Mrs. Henshaw made us laugh when we got back by telling us that a herd of bulls had eaten her glasses, which she had left on the grass beside her book.

FRH and HH were married in Kentville NS Oct. 1st 1921. Two mothers, one father and one sister were there. The bridegroom had dragged his protesting relations to the church well before the appointed hour of eight a.m. and when eight fifteen and then eight-thirty came and went they began to fear the worst. There was of course no telephone handy in those days and no way of sending a message. What had happened was that on this, of all unsuitable days for such a thing to occur, the family car had balked. It started all right and was backed out of the driveway, but there it refused to go forward. An evil omen? The ridiculous sight of father and mother taking the bride to the church, more than a mile away in a car travelling backwards could not be borne. Somehow the problem was eventually solved and the ceremony took place better late than never. It was altogether an unusual wedding party for the feast consisted of a bacon and eggs wedding breakfast and was completely teetotal. The main reason for this early morning ceremony was to allow the bridegroom time to get back on the job at the farm, for this was the season when operation apple was at its peak. So after a hearty breakfast the tin lizzie was loaded up with the bride's luggage, a few last minute presents, like a large fern, and a small yellow kitten. Half way to the farm, sixteen miles from Kentville a gale began, the roof of the car blew straight up the cat escaped and the fern was torn to pieces. What use hurrying to the farm if the wind was going to blow the apple crop to the ground. But somehow the Wavertree orchards escaped and the pickers, packers, graders, truckers etc., all got paid and the happy pair were left to enjoy the excellent domestic arrangements that had been laid on. I well remember that a favorite meal of the family was served — boiled chicken with rice, carrots and white sauce. We have enjoyed it many times since. The weather was perfect and Indian summer temperatures made bathing a delight.

Freddie was always a keen hunter but never had much luck. He decided we must camp out on Blomidon one night and try for a moose. Being most inexperienced I brought the wrong kind of food (sandwiches made of the tail-end of the roast beef, too tough to chew). We were late starting and therefore late arriving at the place where we were going to spend the night and foolishly decided to eat before getting the tent put up. The inevitable happened — it started to rain and we were forced to take shelter in the car where we shivered till daylight and hotfooted it for home — no moose and not even a partridge though we saw quite a few. I must mention the rum — this had been purchased from the local bootlegger at the vast sum of $8.00 and it was so awful that even though we were almost frozen we couldn't drink it.

Freddie always referred to Christmas and New Years as the festering season, but I believe he enjoyed that first Christmas day at Wavertree farm. We had a gathering at noon with dinner served in the big living room; there were about ten grown ups and five children. After the traditional dinner the usual charades and other games were played. How simple it seems to-day for then there was no radio, no TV no electric light — a telephone which was on a party line with all the neighbours who reputedly took up their receivers for every ring. Even skiing was on a simple scale for though we had real skis we had no poles. The roads were not plowed so often there was no possibility of getting to town. Once we snowshoed as far as Canning (six miles) carrying our party clothes on a toboggan and took the train in to Kentville for a dance. The worst time for road travel was in the spring when the frost was coming out. There were then no paved roads in the vicinity and one therefore stayed put.

Fred joined the Kings Canadian Huzzars when he came to the farm and commanded the Canning Squadron. He also managed the Canning hockey team and attended all the games which were held in Wolfville, Kentville and so on. There was a good deal of activity during the winter getting the years supply of firewood down from the mountain, sawn split and piled against the big demand. There were four houses to be supplied. By this time the farm staff was down to Bennett, Huntley and Spencer. The couple, Connors had left and economy measures were in force. A trip to Montreal was made in the slack time after the New Year — this primarily for the relations to inspect the bride, to do some shopping and buy a dog. The dog, an airedale puppy and a huge Oka cheese travelled down with us in the train, in a stateroom. When we alighted at Truro the conductor said "Oh I knew you had it all the time!" We never knew whether he meant the puppy or the cheese!!

Mary Beatrice Henshaw was born in Wolfville on August 28th 1922. She was a very beautiful baby. That autumn Freddie with Alexander for a guide shot a moose back of Windsor. This with a great deal of salt cod became a regular diet for us. A very severe winter with various illness and the tragedy of the death of Mary Beatrice from pneumonia in April 1923 may have been a strong influence in deciding FRH to forsake the life of a farmer and seek a more comfortable way of living. It was his idea to study art in Paris the winter of '23-24, to see whether he had sufficient talent to make a living at it; he also was intrigued with the idea of applying to a friend in the South American country of Equador for employment with the army there. While at Chatham in England a student from Equador had promised his classmates a warm welcome should they decide to come to his country. Before decisive steps had been taken an invitation arrived through Col. Paul Benoit, then DEO in Halifax, for Freddie to come to Halifax for the winter '23-24 to help with Engineer training. For Freddie there was no question but to accept the Army's offer and arrangements were made to move to Halifax where he would start work in October. There he very quickly reentered various phases of army life, including taking part in a play being put on by the Garrison Players. They worked hard and had a lot of fun over "Dandy Dick", including their trip to New Glasgow, where the audience, according to Freddie, consisted of one small boy in the gallery and a few people with complimentary tickets who never cracked a smile, they had a pleasant trip there and back though, travelling in private car "Nova Scotia", provided by Grandfather.

Christmas 1923 was celebrated by dining en famille at the Halifax Hotel, the fashionable social spot of that era. Nova Scotia was dry so people brought their drinks with them; A large party arrived carrying their supplies in a small tin trunk. A family party were the McNeils with their three teen age girls, Janet, Edith and Isobel, very attractive in fluffy party dresses. Before dinner GEG (Grandfather Graham) was looking for his son-in-law and asked Col. [name uncertain: Gamey Jones] if he had seen him, "Why yes," replied Games, "He has just walked through the lobby and up the stairs on his hands".

New Years Eve we dined in the Mess, about thirty mess members and their wives, or things; it was a memorable occasion for me, my first experience of a mess dinner. Everything was so gay and colourful, pretty dresses on pretty girls smart uniforms on good looking males, good dinner, good wine good companions. I was tremendously impressed by the solemnity of the moment of drinking the toast to the King, and the ritual of clearing the table before dessert and the unwritten rule of no smoking in the anterooms for fifteen minutes before dinner was announced.

Freddie, realizing that he liked the army life better than farming, applied to stay on in the Permanent Force, I nearly wrote "Farce" as it was jokingly referred to by some of its members. Having been accepted, he followed Guy Turner's advice and applied to take the Staff Course preparatory course which was held at RMC. Freddie had to do a great deal of swotting to be ready to start the course so we lived a very quiet life in a furnished house in Halifax for the summer of 1924. We moved to Kingston in time for Freddie to join the course Oct. 1st and had comfortable rooms in a boarding house on Sydenham Street. Freddie began to study in real earnest and had a lucky break by having his old friend Ken Stuart on the same course. Ken had been studying for several years and they walked over to the College every morning and Freddie, as he described it, "picked Ken's brains." There was a certain amount of social life, evenings of mah jong, occasional dinners, Sunday teas and we played Badminton Wednesdays and Saturdays in the Armouries. Thanksgiving and Christmas were spent with the Henshaws in Montreal at the Ritz and on Christmas day Freddie entertained a large party of relations at his Club. Uncle Frank (Dr. Shepherd) was there, Aunt Minnie and Aunt Maude and Cousin Vi. Hobart and Del and Ross and Alice. My parents were there too.

The summer of '25 was enlivened by the Strike in Sydney. Letters written by FRH are herewith appended as well as 2 articles he wrote for the Sapper paper.

The journey to Quetta and Staff College, 1925–1928

HALIFAX QUEBEC MONTREAL LIVERPOOL LONDON 1925

This heading needs some explanation. We came by train from Halifax to Quebec, intending to embark there, but it meant going aboard from a tender, so we went to Montreal by train and embarked there quite comfortably, having been seen off by the Grahams, Cousin V Ross and Alice, Hobart and Del, who showered us with parting gifts of books, flowers and fruit etc. This was my first sea voyage and proved quite enjoyable. Freddie had strong reactions from his typhoid shots and was deathly sick coming up in the train and in fact remained so until the bar opened as we progressed down river. He was a splendid tutor to a novice voyageur. At the Ship's concert Freddie recited the "Wreck of the Julie Plante"—he always did—the Master of Ceremonies was the late R.B. Bennett. The wonderful meals, comfortable smoking room, pleasant travelling companions all contributed to make it a very pleasant journey. We went through the Straits and the colouring of land sea and sky was "out of this world."

We arrived at Liverpool very late on the 29th (Nov.). There was snow on the ground and more in the air. After the usual custom delay we boarded the boat train which put us down in London about midnight. Happily there was a sort of small bus, ordered ahead of time, to take us, the baby and our 17 pieces of luggage to the hotel. This had been chosen for us by two of the cousins. It was a ghastly place, but the cousins are now dead and the hotel was bombed so let them all be nameless. It was certainly a grim place, unheated and unlicensed and we arrived a day before we were expected. Everything seemed awful; we were cold thirsty dirty and hungry and as I recollect we crawled into bed under a mountain of puffs and when morning came it brought some cheer in the form of early tea, never more welcome. The sun was now shining, the snow gone, Queen Alexandra's funeral over, though the streets were still draped in their mourning emblems. The first objective was shopping for suitable clothes for Helen Hannah and for her father, who must wear a "bowler" in London. He also had to order tropical service kit, saddles and bridles etc.

Because it was so cold in London Helen and I went down with Freddie to Torquay, where he settled us in a semi-private hotel; it was pretty ghastly and the fellow guests were oh so dull. Here I met jugged hare for the first (and last) time. Freddie went off to Wool to do an attachment to the Tanks and eventually met us on our arrival back in London. He was looking frightfully smart in a new grey spring and fall and bowler and yellow gloves of course. He had terrific news — we were to share a flat with his mother and sister in Queen Annes' Mansions for the rest of our stay. Wonderful old Queen Annes. It was fascinating, from the miles of Turkey carpet to the quaint uniforms worn by the waitresses in the restaurant way up on the top floor. Queen Annes was then the highest building in London. There we saw a variety of people, Hillie Roberts from Halifax who afterwards married "Stoney" — a gunner friend of Pop's — stationed in Kingston, he is supposed to have come back from his honeymoon, got a bit tight in the Mess and retired to bed in what was his old single officers quarter — quite forgetting he now had a wife and a flat to go home to. Mac Shepherd's wife Olive, a ballet dancer from Australia, came for tea and danced for Helen. Then there were the Miss Wintles who lived in a very tall very narrow house where maids ran up and down stairs all day carrying cans of hot water, pots of tea and coals for the many fires. Ruth had some theatrical Kendall friends and we saw Kenneth Johnson, then a medical student. We went to a great many plays and did some sight seeing. A Christmas carol service in Westminster Abbey (just round the corner from Queen Annes) was a memorable occasion. Shopping was fun in the wonderful departmental stores, the Army and Navy being a typical example, and well within walking distance of the Hotel. Christmas was quietly celebrated en famille and on New Years Eve Freddie took me out on the town. First we dined at Hatchetts, a favorite place of his. Then we went to the now demolished Leicester Lounge, hard to describe, but fascinating. Here I felt very conspicuous being the only female visibly enceinte. We drank the popular creme de menthe till time to go by growler—like our Montreal cabs—to Westminster Cathedral, the very modern, and I think ugly RC Cathedral, where we had hoped to stay for the midnight mass. But there were no places to be had so we wandered along to a coffee stall in Parliament square, heard Big Ben boom midnight rushed up to Trafalgar Square to see the crowds and then got ourselves a hansom cab (only 19 left in London) and drove up and down Piccadilly waving and being waved at by fellow celebrants.

There were lots of foggy rainy days, but the grayest of all was Jan 8th when we entrained for Tilbury and embarked in SS Ranpura for the passage to India, which follows in the next chapter.

We sailed from Tilbury in an already crowded ship, which after the usual stormy winter crossing of the Bay of Biscay and a two day stay in Marseilles, made memorable by the three inch snow covering over everything, was bulging at the seams when the overlanders arrived. A marathon from dining room to lounge took place every evening for coffee. I seldom won being far too cumbersome. At table we started off with the family Smith, London born, father mother and daughter, a veritable china doll. Somehow we didn't qualify and they had their places changed. Mrs S distinguished herself by falling down a stairway and gashing her head wide open before Gib. In place of the Smiths we acquired three unattached men, one a doctor who was the life of the party, along with Freddie. I should have already mentioned that we were travelling with Margaret Turner and Malcom age 6, who were going out to join Guy at Quetta. Like me Margaret was deathly seasick for some days during which staunch Freddie dealt with young Malcom, who wasn't a bad kid, but did like quite a bit of affection of the kissing type which was foreign to F. Helen called the boy MAKUM. She was in charge of the nurse Mrs. Yorke, who needs a chapter all to herself. She had queer friends (she was travelling first, having taken up Ruth Henshaw's passage at the last minute) a penchant for Greek Brandy and was colour blind. Helen had lovely clothes but nearly always came on deck in a mauve dress, pink socks and blue sweater. But Mrs. Y had her kind side and in cahoots with the doctor prepared the sick bay for the possible arrival of young Freddie.

Margaret Turner was telling me the other day about the "trouble" she had had on that voyage with one of the ship's officers — I gather that she escaped the fate worse than death. I remember the night of the ship's fancy dress party while watching the dancing a female passenger said "Aren't you afraid this may affect the little unborn?" "This" presumably was the monstrous comic animal heads some characters were wearing.

We had a marvelous view of Stromboli in eruption, because Lord Inchape, chairman of the P&O Board was a passenger we were taken as close as possible to the island and it looked exactly like a terrific great wound with two enormous streams of blood rushing down into the sea, there clouds of steam arose as the blood I mean lava reached the water.

Port Said brought relief from the crowded ship for a large number of people disembarked there.

The usual shipboard romances sprang up — girls going out to get married, changed their minds and arranged to marry the new men. There were deck sports, ably managed by Brian Rigden who afterwards became one of our good friends. Freddie recited "The Wreck of the Julie Plante" as usual at the ship's concert. We played a lot of bridge at which I usually won money. My partner was frequently the aforementioned doctor whose favorite expression was "emboldened by liquor". He was a dear and I only wish I could even remember his name. Early tea, breakfast, elevenses, lunch, tea, cocktail food, sandwiches in the lounge before bed and of course fruit in the cabin kept us from starving to death. Ladies were not welcome in the smoking room in those days — an unwritten rule, one of the things not done.

Clothes were a great problem, especially for me so very preggy. We left London in winter outfits, got into summer things before Port Said, stayed thus until we got nearly up the hill to Quetta where it was freezing cold again. Quetta 6,000 ft up usually has a few sub zero nights every winter. We arrived at the end of January and spring was just around the corner.

Our arrival at Bombay was highlighted by the stupidity of Mrs. Yorke. She left Freddie's pocket watch in the cabin. We had lent it to her for the voyage. By the time we discovered the loss we were at the dock where we were to embark Ventura for Karachi. Mrs. Y was dispatched by taxi back to Ranpura where by sheer force she abstracted the said watch from the steward, who claimed it had been taken away with the soiled linen. We really didn't care and rather hoped that Mrs. Y would miss the boat, but she didn't and we were off to Quetta — another chapter.

An unfortunate outbreak of measles took place aboard Ranpura. Malcolm succumbed by the time we reached Karachi and he and Margaret were hospitalised in a jaillike isolation hospital. Helen had them too undoubtedly but an understanding and sympathetic doctor diagnosed her rash as hives and allowed us to proceed to Quetta where we were immediately quarantined.

The air in Quetta was so intoxicating we scarcely had a drink at first. We walked all over, climbed small mountains and [unclear] the new baby would hurry up & arrive. I went to hospital in the middle of the night by ambulance & left poor Freddie dealing with Mrs Yorke and Helen who at that time was called "Ping" — mahjong being a very popular game and 3 of a kind [unclear]. HHH

To return to the Carsons. I first met them when Fred and Doss came to call on us at Staff College, Quetta, bringing Peter, when Helen was two and little Freddie just born. Doss left soon after to spend the summer with her twin boys in Canada. On her return to Quetta she became seriously ill with enteric and as the critical stage came close, Fred appealed to us, with tears in his eyes, literally, to let the nurse who had been with Doss stay on, rather than come to us for George's birth. It might have proved fatal to change nurses at that time and we were quite content to have Mrs H instead of Miss M. Mrs. H turned out to be a blessing in disguise for not only was she a capable nurse and took good care of the baby and me, but she also saved my horse Red Wing's life. One night a syce brought him to the bungalow obviously in agony. Mrs H who spoke the language like a native, poured half a bottle of brandy down his throat, the horse's not the syce's, sent for the vet and personally supervised the administration of the necessary enema!!!

Ottawa and Military Intelligence, 1928–1929

His appointment as Assistant Director Military Intelligence on his return from Staff College was a real thrill for Freddie; all of the work was interesting and the Editing of the Defence Quarterly just up his alley. Some quotations from letters written by him at this time are most illuminating.

Arriving with children on May 1st we moved into Col Shuldum Hill's house on 4th Avenue. It was a comfortable narrow tall house with tiny garden in the back. Later the landlord put in a second bathroom on the third floor which made it more liveable. The district was not very congenial and we knew no one who lived nearby. Freddie spent two hours daily in the street cars, for he came home for lunch. Later we acquired Aunt Ruth's old Chev which was a great help in every way. We played at tennis, hired horses, went on picnics, though it was hard to find decent beaches for a great deal of the shore line is very rocky in the Ottawa vicinity. While Freddie went to Lennoxville to Senior Militia Staff Course the children and I plus "Nanny", a young girl Mrs. H had had ready for us on our arrival from Vancouver, went to "Skinsport". Later we became international spies and lurked up and down the St. Lawrence taking pictures of bridges and bridge sites for a new volume of statistics for the War Office. F. sent in one picture showing me on bridge which was refused. Mustn't show any female figure in the War Office pamphlet. We eventually got to Albany (in which city I was later to have been stated to have been born, for purposes of George's enlistment in the US Army.) There at a very swell hotel we had the royal suite and when F asked the Bell boy if liquor were procurable he was told that good Scotch was $12.00 a bottle. This seemed outrageous in those days, any way we had brought our own supply. Coming back we had a breakdown in the drive shaft near Richelieu and had to stay there two days waiting for a new part. It was a real French Canadian country hotel with lots of pea soup and raisin pies.

Coming home F was driving and apparently exceeded the speed limit of 8 mph which was the rule then and still is (I believe) in a part of Beaconsfield. Aunt Ruth, holidaying with her mother in Victoria was surprised to receive a summons and a notice of fine.

In the autumn of this year Freddie made a camping trip from Cochrane with a guide named Fennimore. They were after moose but were unlucky, not even seeing one. They were also unlucky in that Fennimore broke his ankle and F had to carry him over a long portage; this is mentioned in the chapter on camping trips.

By this time we had joined a dancing club, which was most enjoyable. Of the various members I recall there was Ernie Sansom (gay widower then) the Vernons, Eardley Wilmots, McKeans (David and Mary, a bride), Cargills, Snowdons, Phil & Mary Bakers, and many others. We met twice a month & usually dined [unclear] before the dance. We also [unclear]

Looking back on the year and a half we spent in Ottawa it appears that there was a great deal of social activity. This is true but at the same time lots of evenings and weekends were spent by F in study and work. He frequently brought stuff home from his office and spent long hours in the upstairs sitting room over articles and reviews.

A second spying trip was taken by Freddie alone; he went as far west as Chicago and found it very tiring driving so far alone. In Chicago he was nearly arrested for driving too slowly, the minimum there on their beautiful Lakeshore Drive was then 18 mph.

It was disappointing to Freddie to be transferred to Kingston, after only a year and a half in the Intelligence Branch, which he loved. It happened that "Andy" McNaughton was coming to Ottawa as CGS and he had had Ken Stuart with him on the West Coast, where he became a great admirer of Ken's and therefore had him transferred to Ottawa, to the job which had been Freddie's.

Kingston, 1929–1932

The move to Kingston from Ottawa was accomplished with a great deal of ease. We drove, still with Ruth's car, three children, two maids and our personal luggage, arriving a very short time before the van got there. The night before we had slept in our house in Ottawa and by bedtime we were well settled in the house in Kingston. It is a splendid way to move household goods. George caused a minor flap by disappearing as soon as we arrived at 47 George Street, but a kindly neighbour went to find him; he was having a look at the nearby park. The house was delightful, large drawing room with fireplace, office-cum sitting room ditto. Dining room not very attractive but adequate and it looked alright at night. There were lots of pantries and cupboards and a big kitchen. Upstairs Freddie quickly got to work making a wonderful day nursery. This room had a fireplace and he had blackboards put along one wall, with a frieze of wild animal wall paper above. There was an enormous doll's house, big enough for several children to get in at once. As usual, we persuaded the landlord to instal a second bathroom which made the house much more comfortable. We were well served by two girls from Ottawa. Though being District Engineer Officer was not exactly Freddie's favorite cup of tea, the opportunity to do staff work that occurred from time to time was welcome and enjoyable. He did quite a bit of travelling about and made friends in Peterborough, Picton, Lindsay etc. He spent two weeks in Ottawa running a school and lived at the old Bytown Inn, then a very nice place to stay, and pleasantly reasonable.

One of the first things Freddie had done at 47 George Street was a fence to keep the young ones from escaping into the street. The Brigadier, [name uncertain: Wilboa Andeson] said "Why don't you tell them to stay in the yard". What a question. Freddie said he was afraid the lumber and workmen came from the Army, he had never got round to paying for the fence; however, the army got lots of free service from him and had our boat and outboard forever.

F had purchased a Peterborough freight Canoe and outboard motor and with various friends had had a most enjoyable time going for picnics and fishing excursions.

This aforementioned boat and outboard were a great delight to Freddie; he used them on every possible occasion, taking parties of friends out on afternoon expeditions or on Sundays. Two of these outings were near tragedies. The first, with the Penhales and Morg Morrison and little Helen, was a trip to Kingston Mills on a very fine Sunday. We picnicked and sun bathed and had a smooth journey back as far as the causeway, and here F made an error. He should have disembarked his passengers at the Gunner Mess and beached the canoe there, but it was hard to estimate the size of the waves which were really mountainous by the time we approached the Locomotive works. The canoe was now rising and falling back alarmingly and it seemed as if it would break its back or capsize altogether. We were soaking wet and frightfully cold but made it safely to the Yacht Club dock. We had one non-swimmer and Helen aged six and the water was freezing cold.

Another hair raising trip was coming back from Simcoe Island where we had gone for a Saturday afternoon's fishing with John & Dumpie — no children this time. Again the waves were mountainous and this time we had to cross that wide expanse of lake to reach the Yacht Club. J & D were old hands at accidents, near accidents and hairbreadth escapes so they must have been our salvation — their weight was in helping to hold the canoe steady on her course. I believe Freddie saved us by his magnificent handling of the canoe.

It was while we were in Kingston that the trip "To Ontario's Sea Coast by Canoe" was undertaken. I have quoted it in full herewith and appended some remarks of my own.

Camping and canoe journeys

The outdoor life always held great attraction for F and he seized every opportunity to go camping, or hunting. He and the late "Toby" Jones, of Halifax, who were school friends, made an expedition by canoe on the Mira River, herewith illustrated. With his parents and sister a week's outing was spent at the Robertsons "Shanty". This was up the Ottawa a bit from St. Placide, perhaps nearer St Andrews East. I believe the Robertsons had it for many years as a base for duck shooting expeditions. It is no longer in their possession but the Henshaw children will remember holidays spent there.

In the summer of 1909 F and his father took a camping trip on the Mississippi river, starting from Almonte, where they were living. This was particularly enjoyable to the boy who adored his father and revelled in their companionship. It was to be their last outing together for in September of that year his father succumbed to the accident which befell him when he tried to help some people get their possessions from a burning house. He was struck by a falling beam and fatally injured.

While stationed in Ottawa F journeyed up north and with a guide went after a moose; there was no moose but the trip lived very vividly in his memory because of the plight he found himself in when, sitting round the camp fire and lapping up a few rums (drowning their sorrows over the poor hunting) the guide tripped over a log and broke his ankle. This meant that F had to carry him out over a long and rough portage; luckily the guide, Fennimore, was absolutely minute. More of him later.

The next trip was a most ambitious one and I can do no better than quote verbatim from F's article in the Defence Quarterly of [unclear] "To Ontario's Sea Coast by Canoe". [The complete Defence Quarterly article referred to here has not been found among these scans.]

A very few notes, from my point of view may amuse. To wit:— Fennimore, who had never taken females on that kind of trip before was quite sceptical about my inclusion in the party, especially as I was passenger in his canoe. His oft repeated admonition was "set still, set still." The matter of the tides was a complete mystery to this woodsman. He had often been to the Bay, and always the tide had been in when he arrived, early afternoon! His fodder was wonderfully satisfying and he had a way with camp cooking that was out of this world. One more item. F spoke very firmly to me about not carrying any heavy loads over the long portages. I therefore found myself draped with the ax, camera, guitar, fishing equipment, personal kit bag, maps, notebooks, liquor supply (meagre) and what have you.

A simple forty pound bag of flour secured by tump line would have been an easier and less skittery scatterly load. Of our four days in Mattice, waiting in the small hotel for our supplies to arrive, (they had been sent by freight instead of Express by mistake.) they can be summed up in the hotelkeeper's remark, "Of course it is not the Windsor Hotel in Montreal, but we'll do our best for you."

Camping trips — Up the Rideau 193[not supplied]

While still stationed in Kingston a trip up the Rideau was undertaken accompanied by the two boys aged [unclear].

The first night of this trip was quite in character. We were visited by a terrific thunder storm with torrential rain. Freddie, always a smart camper, had the tent up and everything stowed away before dark and the advent of the deluge. The camp site was a good one and we sheltered warm and dry. The water we had to use was slime covered but boiled did us no harm. This trip remains in memory as a very pleasant one; we did some sailing but mostly proceeded by our small but sturdy outboard. This motor remained a distinct adjunct until lost to us in the mists of time, army and Petawawa and war. It suffered the usual vicissitudes of its kind, fell off and sank in a deepish part of the Ottawa River near the camp bathing area. Recovered by the diving efforts of Alex McTavish and other sappers we used it one year for surf boarding in a very amateurish way. Back to our camping trip however. We went as far as Smith Falls, there to do some shopping, both wet and dry. On the return voyage a pleasant camping site was found on one of the larger lakes. There the boys and the dog, Doddie, were highly entertained by seeing what we thought was either a snrog or a Frake. A small snake had caught a small frog in its mouth and half swallowed it — result something like this.

A really super camping site above Jones Falls was discovered on our return journey and we later went there and set up a proper camp site; there F painted the famous scene with canoe. This picture offered for sale at a VON Art Show was purchased at a vastly over-valued price by Aunt Ruth, then given back to Riversmead. It all helped the VON mightily.

Camping Trips

In 1935 at the conclusion of the Camp Schools at Petawawa F took two weeks leave and we embarked on a camping trip up the Ottawa. After weekending with the Beaments at Fort William we set sail (literally, see picture below) and proceeded up stream at good speed. As usual the elements frowned on our venture and an approaching thunder storm of some magnitude forced us to take shelter in the gallery of a cottage, empty, near the bottom of the trail to Oiseau Rock. It really was a doozer of a storm but we managed to batten down the canoe and get our stores under cover before it really got rough. The high rock cliffs behind our shelter made the noise doubly frightening and nerve wracking. We were too tired to go further after the storm abated and dossed down where we were—much to our embarrassment the owners of the cottage, whom we did not know, appeared early in the morning, having come out by motor boat from Pembroke to see if their cottage had been damaged in the storm. We quickly got our stuff together and proceeded up stream. It is a very beautiful part of the river and skirting Oiseau Rock where the river is terrifically deep (as it is opposite Como) we crossed over to the Ontario side and found a fine camping site where we remained a day or two. Our best camp site was on the Gillies property near [place left blank]. Here we explored a fascinating river, bathed in the nude, fished and loafed. Our only visitors were some curious cows who looked in during the night.

This part of the Ottawa River may be quite changed now but in those days there were plenty of fine beaches to be found, where bathing was excellent and often springs were encountered near the camp site. Young Freddie became famous for his oft repeated "If only I'd brought my paints". He spent much time sitting gazing at some particularly paintable scene while George was toiling up from the shore helping to carry the camping equipment. Later George became most proficient in making the breakfast porridge and caring for the fire.

Camping trips — Horseshoe Island — near Kingston — 1937

During our stay in Kingston F arranged for an RMC motor boat to take us to this small island which lies beyond Wolfe Island and is quite uninhabited but for some cattle pastured there from time to time and the island's tremendous population of snakes, large, small, fat thin and horrible. We four, Freddie, George Barbara and I, went down on Friday with an adequate amount of food and necessary equipment. We were visited on Saturday afternoon by Johny Berminham and Tiff Macklen who were fishing in Johny's favorite spot off an old wharf on Simcoe. They had caught a few nice fish and we were delighted to be given a mess. The boat came for us on Sunday afternoon; I wonder what would have happened to us if anyone had become suddenly ill or an accident occurred; I expect Freddie would have made some signalling device, or one of us could have swum to Wolfe Island and gone for help.

Halifax and Petawawa in the 1930s

The first summer after moving to Halifax was somewhat disjointed. We started off in rooms at Hillside Hall, moved to Kingsport and lived with the Cox'; then back to Ontario to Petawawa, to Fisher's Farm. Returning to Nova Scotia, we established ourselves in the Cottage at Kingsport, Freddie being stationed at Aldershot. I believe this was the summer F bought the Sable Island pony, having decided it would be a fine thing for the kids to have. It was wild, and useless for children, and its classic act was to bite, not the HAND that fed it but the WAPPUS. As so often happens father buys something for the kids but is not on hand to take care of it and that chore naturally falls on mother. This animal, Winsome Winnie he was called regardless of sex, had to have a stable, found in the village, had to be cleaned fed and watered and somehow exercised. I may say he didn't last long in the Henshaw menage. I recall having the canoe and a raft there. The raft was useful but the canoe seldom used as the high and low tides made its use very limited. It was, like the pony, more nuisance value for it often had to be either hauled up or down in a gale and it was no light weight for woman and children. All this sounds very complaining but it is just an attempt to jot down notes regarding that phase of our home life.

In the autumn we were able to get into our quarters at Wellington Barracks; These were very spacious and all enjoyed living there. One drawback, the height (about 100 ft it seemed) and danger of children falling out of windows, when between seasons of storm windows or screens. Soon we became fully occupied with attendance at games of soccer, basket ball, softball and badminton. We played badminton in the old Military gym on Cogswell Street Wednesdays and Saturdays and sometimes one evening a week. Later on Sunday mornings we took the kids to coach them. Still later I played in a morning club (F encouraged this because some of the members were amusing and he got a laugh when I came home and recounted the morning's sayings and doings).

F was very assiduous in his duties; he never missed church parade when the Company had to be there. One occasion church parade was called for on the day the Ski Club was having an outing, by train, to Wentworth Valley. But he wouldn't slope off, his belief being that if the men were ordered to go to church the least the CO could do was go too. After all, church parades then were only once a month. The skiing at Old Sackville was quite good enough for us and we spent many happy days there. The Halifax Ski Club acquired an old farm house for a shelter and there we would have our lunch, usually accompanied by a few Hertzbergs, Vinces children and Dunbar girls. Sometimes we eat out of doors, down by the Sackville River, having run the old wood road trail, most precipitous!! There was an alternative route, not so steep but ending in a short sharp run down to the River, this was christened Henshaw Trail. It very seldom had enough snow to cover the rocks and wasn't much used by us. The picture below is of the old Halifax Ski Club. When there was no snow we all went skating, sometimes on [unclear].

Freddie was a great one for organising the Sunday lunchers into parties of hounds and hares or cowboys and Indians and [unclear] down to the Park for our games. We also spent some time in the other direction, behind Bedford where the Dept. Nat. Def. had property which Freddie was looking over for some reason. Influenced by the Walshs (Geof and Gwynne) of [unclear] we attempted many fishing outings; usually these were unsuccessful as far as fish were concerned but good fun just the same. One spring weekend we hiked in over a pretty rough trail and camped out at Silver Lake. The Walshs caught fish, but not even George got any.

We moved to a larger quarter, at the end of the Barracks, where we had ample room and a bit of a garden—mostly used to hang out clothes to dry. A new office building was built for the RCSME and for dry and wet canteens.

The annual trek to Petawawa made life interesting and relieved any previous staleness. The Field Company usually started off about May 24th, as "Daugh" pointed out to me in Aldershot, England, in 1940. We were left behind till school finished and then we hotfooted it to join Freddie in Petawawa.

For two years we rented a super cottage on the Petawawa River, complete with Guest cabin. No indoor plumbing however. One year we stayed at Sikorskis boarding house up the road. This was quite comfortable but we needed a place of our own so that Freddie could bring down chaps from camp and the children spread themselves a bit. In 1939, at the end, we had staying Marydel, George Ross, Bunty Craig, Miriam Smith Connie Vokes, Gwynne Walsh and her mother and [name uncertain: Gwn Mztin]. Not all these at once. Bathing in the Petawawa was wonderful and at our door. It was during our stay in the cottage that Freddie did his famous dog-food-eating act. He got home while we were away swimming at the Point and feeling hungry and thirsty he got our cold beer (we Drank beer in those days!!) and what he thought was some cooked hamburg for a sandwich. He enjoyed his snack and was no whit put out when we told him he had been eating dogfood!!!

The annual Army Navy hockey game was always good for a laughing afternoon. The Navy had some very good hockey players at that time, Len Murray, Dutchy Edwards, to name a couple. The referee was always dear old Gavin Rainnie, killed at Dieppe. He would turn out in an extraordinary kit, consisting of top hat, big RMC sweater and some sort of fancy pants. F always was on the team but not often on the ice for he was not a very fast skater. One year Alex McTavish provided the laughs. He had literally never been on skates before and his antics made everyone think he "had had one over the eight". It was claimed by the Army when they lost, that the Navy had a drinking team and a playing one, to attend the convivial lunch in the mess before the game.

Mobilisation and England, 1939–1941

After the Fd Coy arrived back in Halifax life became a mad rush. Recruiting began in real earnest and all the chaps were getting married. F. was busy day and night. He was best man several times and father of the bride at least once. We said goodbye to our friends in the RCR before they went off to Valcartier to join the rest of the Regiment. The death of my mother and attendant obligations took up quite a bit of time. There were quite a few parties and a sort of eve of Waterloo gaiety. People like Johny Birmingham telephoned begging F to get him into the Fd Coy. This was "Schmid's" pigeon not F's.

I think F was very happy at this time doing a job he liked, one they had been training for for years. Later we heard that Gen McNaughton questioned F's appointment as OC FD Coy, he had heard "that Henshaw was drinking a lot". Both Schmidlin and Charlie Hertzberg (RIP) assured Andy that F wouldn't let him and First Div down and he didn't. So word came that he was to revert to major and go overseas in command of the First of the Field.

The advance party First Div sailed away, Des McGee with them. We were left with his Buick and Neel Hodson's as well as our Chev. Our place looked like a used car lot. The 1st and 2nd Contingents of first div left. We spent a quiet family Christmas.

The day of embarkation arrived and the Field Coy marched through the streets to the docks; the "little wives" and I drove from corner to corner to catch every possible glimpse of them without actually marching alongside, holding hands and clinging as the Halifax kin of the sappers did. Tommy Williams went about taking pictures.

F very kindly let the little wives have their husbands ashore till the very last minute. He and the wifeless did their work.

On the Sunday Barb and the boys came down to have a last glimpse of their Daddy but he didn't appear — obviously he was much too busy to be hanging over the side of the ship nattering to his family.

A friend in the know told me the ETD and once more the little wives and I hot footed it down to the dock. The girls soon found their men and Jean and George had a ship to shore conversation almost within touching distance. Sappers leaning over the rail in the bow said I'd find F at the stern, but there I was told he'd be midships, near Spencer. But he didn't appear and I wasn't too sad because I was counting on meeting him in London within two weeks. When we met in London he explained his non appearance when they sailed—their departure coincided with the opening of the bar which he could not miss!!

The Fd Coy went to [name uncertain: Talaferra] Barracks on arrival at Aldershot. It was still pretty miserably cold, but the worst of the winter was over when the plumbing didn't work and the tiny fires were quite inadequate to heat the big rooms of the Barracks. There was five day landing leave and F came to London and joined me at the Cumberland Hotel. I half expected him to want to tear up to Scotland to do a walking tour, but he was contented to stay and frivol in London Town. This we did with the Lutons, Penhales, Chris Vokes, Pat Kelly, Steve Morres and Janet Carruthers; the latter was joining the MTC and brought her uniform for our inspection, khaki cotton stockings, shirts, ties, and even khaki underwear. Her jackets and skirts were being made to order by Paquin!!! F suggested that I try to join the MTC too but I pointed out that I had deserted my children in order to be with him in England and not to pursue a war career of my own.

Back at Aldershot I stayed in the quaint Victoria Hotel. A large blackboard in the hall listed room numbers, alongside of which occupants chalked their breakfast orders. We saw old friends and went to some mess parties. At the RCR we met Dorothy Albrecht; when F was presented to her she exclaimed "Oh you are the man who eats dog food." It was true for one day when F arrived at the Cottage at Petawawa, hungry, he found an inviting looking dish of what he thought was cooked hamburg; he made and ate a substantial sandwich and pronounced it damn good.

Dorothy very quickly proved herself a true friend to Canadians. Not very long after this she had the Sprys, Mathers, F and I, as well as her son and herself staying in her house, as well as the two known as GusanNeel, Taschereau and Hodson (RCR).

The Coy moved to Pangbourne for river training and were quartered in one of the larger stately homes in a beautiful park. Basildon Park and Hall belonged to the Ferdenandez family. Father and mother were living in the Dower House, ugly and small but cosy and they gave us a beautiful dinner there. Their son and daughter in law, Sally, a deep-south youall American, lived in a converted stable within the brickwalled kitchen gardens. They were very friendly and gave a wonderful cocktail party in another of their houses, a ranch type beautifully furnished one, which was closed for the duration.

Back in Aldershot again at Talaferra we took a small cottage at Cove with the Aitkens. Bob was due to go to France almost at once so he and Nell rated the double bedded master bedroom with fireplace. After Bob left Nell was pretty sad and twitchy. One morning, about 6 a.m. F's driver decided to wash the windows of the station wagon and as each window was finished he banged the door shut. Nell stood three windows and then leant from her casement window and in a scathing tone (her very very scathing) said "IF you have to wash your car at this hour of the morning please do it under some other window."

ENGLAND in 1940
The Dagger Thrust at the Heart of Berlin

About the first day of May the balloon went up for the First Field Coy. They were ordered to proceed to what one of the NCO's assured me was Southampton—but for South read North; they were to embus at midnight and the evening was spent in the officers' quarters, where F allowed the little wives to spend all the available time with their husbands. Long before zero hour we began to harness F into his kit, and it took the combined efforts of his batman, driver adjutant and me to hoist him into the front seat of his lorry once he was fully laden; then he couldn't raise his arm to look at his watch to synchronize time of departure. An appended letter from F. tells how they got lost following what they thought was the convoy's tail. What he didn't mention was the rage of the RAF OC of the airfield in the middle of which they found themselves, come the cold grey dawn.

A report of an accident to a cyclist of the Hasty P's which was received at Red Cross Headquarters in London, where I was presently working, plus a telephone call from F when the operator told me quite plainly that the call came from Kettering, jelled my decision to become a camp follower. So getting leave from Mrs. H I dashed to Swan & Edgar, bought blouse and coat and skirt, tie and a sort of uniform hat, all navy blue including stockings. Up Regent street at [unclear] blue oxfords were obtained. All in all a creditable uniform. Then a mad dash to Paddington where I was surprised to find Mary Plow and Rosamund Crowe and a spaniel dog as fellow passengers. They were going to stay with friends but I was on my own to find a place to stay as well as finding Freddie. I bused from Northampton and got out at a village where I saw some Signallers stringing wire. They directed me to the Field Coy where I got a lift and went to visit the Hasty P in hospital not far away. He was still unconscious but the staff were very glad to have me go to his company and get his papers, which had not yet arrived. When Freddie had some free time we went across the road to a little pub where we had dinner and I stayed the night; but since F could have very little time off I went back to London early the next morning and back to Berkeley Square to work. I was given quite a bit of praise for my efforts on behalf of the Hasty P. I heard later that General McNaughton was furious when he spotted a group of wives congregated on a street corner in a nearby village.

On May 24th the Fd. Coy went off on the "Bust to Brest". As the train pulled out of the station [name uncertain: Daughinee], "Daugh", leaned from a window and called out to me "Well Mrs. Henshaw we always leave on the 24th of May and I hope we'll all get back safely as we've done before." They did that time. Mary Plow telephoned to say they were coming back and I'm afraid my reply led her to believe I wasn't too pleased; it wasn't that I didn't want F back again I just felt sad for them that they had to come running home with their tails between their legs. Frustrating. Connelly also phoned to tell me they were going to Bourassa Barracks instead of back to Tal---- He also told me the ETA of the train so Dorothy and [unclear] Bell hied us down to Farnborough station in the cold grey dawn and were there when the train crept in. Not a sign of life at first then a few sleepy looking soldiers peered out the windows. Capt. Bell, RCR Medical officer descended and wrapped his wife in the all time record of public hugging and kissing. Soon troops were detraining and I was able to tell F of Connelly's message; the RTO hadn't word of it and told them to go to Tal. but when they did some phoning they discovered the truth and so the Coy. had a much shorter march to make and at the end found a hot meal awaiting. I preened, but heard later that Bill Hodson, OC [unclear], not too well, disgruntled and unhappy, seeing three females on the platform said to his son Neel, one of his Coy officers, "These women anything to do with you?" The RCR went to a rather unpleasant camp site but they still came to D's in Farnborough for succor (drink and food and female company!!). D & Gus and F and I had a braw day in London when they went to order new kit; for all they owned except what they were wearing was left behind in France. I always regretted F's beautiful blue silk pyjamas and the blue soup cups I had presented to the Fd Coy mess had to fall into the German hands.

RMC and Helen’s marriage, 1941–1943

When F arrived back from England in July 1941 there was no job ready for him at the College; he was given a desk in [name uncertain: Archie Campbell]'s office and began to do some work for Col Lowther on the Senior Officers Classes. Colonel Lowther was transferred and FRH became OC of the Course, running nine successful courses, until he was retired in June 1943. He had a grand lot of instructors to work with and I believe he had a very happy time during the year and a half or a bit more that he was at the College.

Undoubtedly his friends, Hertzberg, Commandant RMC and Stuart, Chief of Staff, were instrumental in getting FRH this very pleasant job. It included a very fine quarter at the College to which the family moved in the spring of '42. Previously we lived in a small unfireplaced flat on Johnston Street, adequate but not very cosy. Barbara went to Sydenham School that year. Helen came home in April, just after the move to RMC, which was a good thing as she brought two college pals and as RMC was still functioning as a cadet college there were lads to entertain the glamour pusses.

The circumstances surrounding Helen's meeting with Jim Hunter always seem most romantic; Jim was learning to fly at Collins Bay. FRH had played a good deal of chess with Vaughan Morris RAF coming back in the ship; this officer was coming out to be adjutant at Collins Bay, an Empire training station for the Fleet Air Arm and Air Force. They renewed acquaintance at the RMC boxing tournament and eventually gravitated back to quarters. There was to be an Engineer Womens' Auxiliary dance we were sponsoring and the girls needed escorts so Vaughan agreed to provide same. The girls expected bell-bottom trousers but to their surprise a party of subs arrived — all rather short except one — Helen chose him. Next day, he and two friends were paddling under the causeway just as the girls were on their way to town, greetings were exchanged and later they "just happened to meet" in the popular eating place, Green Room. Helen phoned to ask permission to invite them for supper which of course was granted and so that was the first meeting of Helen and Jim. The other lad was Ray Oakes, Jim's pal.

Fortunately there was an adequate meal in preparation, we generally seemed to eat well in those days and usually had people to help in the kitchen. This was made possible by a plan worked out by Mary Dalton. In return for room and board a wife and one or two small children, or none at all, gave her services in the household while the husband was stationed in Kingston, either at Vimy or some other Barracks. We were lucky to have Margaret and Leo Dupuis during the time he was in Kingston with the RCME. We had one frightful scare during their residence when the younger baby fell into a very boiling hot bath and got quite badly burned and had to be hurried to hospital. But he was a rugged type and survived. One night, later on, Helen, hurrying in to fetch the carrots crashed through the glass part of the pantry door and cut her hands and ruined the carrots — with butter on too. Leo got into a jam at the end, Margaret went home with the kids just before he was due to leave with a draft for overseas. His last night he got mixed up in a party and overslept and missed his train. I never saw a sadder young soldier than poor Leo sitting in the kitchen writing an explanatory letter to his wife. I have never heard anything of them since.

To return to the Hunter-Henshaw courtship. It had the approval of her parents and as he was of sufficient rank and seniority for permission to marry to be a mere formality, or perhaps that was because it was in the War, in any case, they were very soon affianced. It was very funny when Jim asked Pop for Helen's hand. Pop said, "let's have a drink" and then suggested going out to acquaint the females that the seal of approval had been placed on the engagement. In the garden they found the bride to be swinging like a monkey on a heavy rope attached to a large branch high up in the huge tree, of course with A ROPE not her tail. So plans were made for an early wedding and it was decided to have it in Como. There were splendid pictures taken to tell the rest of the story.

F looked particularly smart at the wedding in blue patrol jacket.

Riversmead and family weddings

On May 19th 1944 we moved into Riversmead and it was undoubtedly a most thrilling moment, being the first time we had a home of our very own since 1923 when we left Wavertree Farm in Nova Scotia. The house was full of places to investigate, bound to fire the imaginations of the two teen agers we had at home, George and Barb. Cousin Edna had left a substantial amount of furniture behind, beds, bureaus, tables, kitchen cupboard and above all two almirahs (wardrobes). But there were some alterations necessary and these were carried out during the early years of our occupancy. We acquired three bathrooms and a wonderful back porch, a supposedly ratproof cellar space and the railing from the lower gallery was taken away. Grapevine gradually disappeared and odd flower beds and shrubs dotted about the lawns were turned into lawn and the flowers confined into border beds of rectangular shape. There was a wonderful place for vegetables, especially early. One year we were eating green peas etc on June 1st, having planted same on April 15th. Gradually the kitchen garden took shape and is now a lovely place; at one stage it definitely got too large, it defied the instructions "Never plant more garden than your wife can take care of". Raspberry canes flourished and the crop was terrific; it is supposed that one can clear a thousand dollars an acre from the berries. Perhaps we will reach that stage one day.

At a birthday party that Jim Hunter gave for his wife in Halifax at the L.N Hotel two of the guests said they were coming to Mtl to spend their leave, so Helen suggested that they come to Como for a weekend, where the rule was open house, particularly for service personnel. Jim Hunter said "you'll love it, she's mad and he drinks".

When Paul and Barbara got married the RC Church didn't allow the ceremony to take place in the church proper if one of the contracting parties was non RC. So Pop always claimed that Barbara was not well and truly married since the wedding took place in the Priest's house. Father Cloutier did everything possible to make it a happy occasion and arranged to have his curate play and sing "Oh Promise Me", in English — this curate was very much a tenor. The reception this time was at Riversmead, attended by Paul's Family and Barb's, which then included Aunt Ruth and Grandfather. [name uncertain: Ancy Gundry] was then a staunch helper on any and all occasions. (Even to celebrating Mousa's birthday with her, Pop and to quote Grandfather "a girl called Jean Cote") There was real Fizz and thunder flashes, laid on by Freddie, who happened to be here and busy getting in love with Naomi Ritchie.

Illness, death and obituary, 1959

It seems unnecessary to go over the details of Freddie's illness and to describe the treatments carried out. In hospital for two months, in great discomfort if not actual pain, he displayed great courage, self discipline and consideration for others that were characteristics of his amiable nature. From his return to Riversmead, on March 4th, until his death at the end of August, weekly visits were made to the test centre at the Montreal General Hospital, where the staff were most considerate and kindly. It seems somewhat sad to think that on his last trip to the hospital, on a very hot day, Tuesday August 18th, wanting a refreshing drink of either gin & orange or plain orange juice, they refused to serve him in the car and of course he wasn't up to going into the restaurant. He had been a spending customer there for years.

The funeral took place on Wednesday 26th (Small Paul's birthday) and burial was in St. Mary's churchyard. It was impossible to carry out his wish to be buried up the farm; it required a special license which had to be applied for from Quebec. But through the great efforts of Moser and David the kind of coffin he had requested was made in time. Quite a few Sapper friends came from Ottawa, Kingston and Montreal.

The Brunet Company made a very fine headstone, their one mistake being to make a stroke instead of a dot between Lieut and Col. The Engineer crest was very well done, the workman is evidently an artist. A brass memorial tablet was dedicated on Dec. 13th (his birthday) and placed on the window ledge below the Henshaw Memorial window.

No. 801 Lieutenant-Colonel Frederick Robert Henshaw M.C., psc, R.C.E. (Ret'd)

Lt.-Col. F. R. Henshaw was born in Montreal in 1890 and was educated at R.M.C. from which he graduated in 1911. He spent two years at the R.S.M.C., Chatham, England. He served overseas from 1915 to 1919 and on his return to Canada became a farmer in Nova Scotia. In 1923 he was invited to rejoin the Permanent Force and served with the Royal Canadian Engineers until he went to the Staff College in Quetta, Baluchistan. In 1929 he was Assistant Director of Military Intelligence and Editor of the Defence Quarterly. Later, while District Engineer Officer in Kingston Ontario, he, with the late Colonel "Bill" Wilgar and others, was instrumental in carrying out a re-organization of the Corps, which resulted in better training for Militia Engineers.

Lt. Col Henshaw reverted to Major in order to go overseas in command of the 1st Field Company, took part in the famous "Bust to Brest" and was subsequently promoted to Lt. Col. Returning to Canada in 1941, Lt. Col. Henshaw was posted to R.M.C., where he was in charge of the Senior Officers' Courses until 1943. He retired in that year and again became a farmer at Riversmead, Como P. Que. He died on 23 August 1959, at Como and is survived by his widow, the former Helen Graham, two sons and two daughters.

Remembering Fred Carson, 1960

The death of Fred Carson in Montreal on May 3rd 1960 brings many memories to mind of an association of the Carson and Henshaw families over the years. Freddie told me that he determined to join the Army after seeing Fred, home on leave from the REs, riding on the Lower Common in Kingston. Freddie wanted to join British Infantry, but Ken Stuart, one of his classmates, persuaded him, among others, to try for Canadian Engineers. They were accepted, for which I have always thanked God, because, had Freddie become a BBI he would undoubtedly have been killed early on in the First War, before his group of CEs got overseas. (They were kept on and on in Canada doing defences and training until Artie Lawson, who had camped on the steps of NDHQ, got them started overseas.) Without Freddie, none of my lovely and intelligent children and grandchildren would have been born!!

Doss and I had female babies, Pat and Barbara, in Kingston General Hospital, on practically the same day; the girls met again when we came back to Kingston in 1941 when they were classmates at Sydenham School. By the time Peter left RMC to join the REs in 1942, Doss and I were working at the Blood Clinic, where she did a wonderful job. She must have been under a terrific strain, with Fred in the East and the three boys somewhere abroad.

During the summer of 1942 George Henshaw, 14, delivered groceries for a shop on Barrie Street and sometimes was delivering Lady Carson's order at the back door as his mother arrived at the front for tea.

I have been told that before Doss died in 1944, when Fred had come back and they had some happy time together, that she regained much of her earlier beauty and gaiety. Freddie once told me that Dorothy Brownfield was the prettiest girl at her coming-out June Ball.

When Fred came to live in Montreal, with his sister Vera and Pat and the ever faithful Nanna, we used to see him fairly often, dining with him or having him for Sunday lunch at Riversmead, with such of his family as were about. We had one classic day when Fred and Nadine came out for Hunt races, but latterly our meetings seemed to be confined to chance encounters in banks, airports, railway station and clubs. The last time Fred and Freddie and I were together was in '57 at the RMC Reunion. Now they are both gone, two very gallant Sapper soldiers.
